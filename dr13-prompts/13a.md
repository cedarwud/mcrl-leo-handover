## Conclusion

**Keep the interaction residual as the target for now, but do not spend more compute on the current representation until you test feature sufficiency and the selector independently.** Your formulation has an established mathematical interpretation; it is not inherently broken because it is a residual. The more immediate risks are **information destroyed by aggregation, an incorrect assumption about interaction order, and a selection rule that can favor large coalitions even when the interaction head learns nothing**. Anchored decompositions and Möbius representations provide the relevant foundations. ([PubsOnLine][1])

Two corrections materially change the diagnosis:

* **Nonlinear Deep Sets can represent pairwise interactions.** A fixed scalar compression of pairwise inputs is a different—and potentially fatal—restriction.
* **Your residual is not generally a second-order interaction.** For coalitions larger than two, it contains all interaction orders above one.

Below, published findings are cited; the algebraic identities and counterexamples are direct derivations. The diagnosis of your implementation remains conditional because its evaluator, complete feature map, and selection code are not available here.

## 1. Is residual regression the right formulation?

### 1.1 Your target is an anchored interaction remainder

Fix the context, anchor, and **one proposed alternative action per agent**, and write

$$
v(S)=F(a_S,a^0_{-S}).
$$

The Möbius coefficients are

$$
m(T)=\sum_{S\subseteq T}(-1)^{|T|-|S|}v(S),
\qquad
v(A)=\sum_{T\subseteq A}m(T).
$$

Thus \(m(\varnothing)=v(\varnothing)\), \(m(\{i\})=d_i\), and

$$
\boxed{\Psi(A)=
\sum_{\substack{T\subseteq A\\|T|\ge2}}m(T).}
$$

These coefficients are associated with **Harsanyi dividends** in cooperative games; the corresponding multivariate construction is an **anchored decomposition**, closely related to **cut-HDMR**. These are useful literature search terms for your problem. ([PubsOnLine][1])

For a pair, \(\Psi(\{i,j\})=m(\{i,j\})\). For a triple,

$$
\Psi(\{i,j,k\})
=m(\{i,j\})+m(\{i,k\})+m(\{j,k\})+m(\{i,j,k\}).
$$

Consequently, **pairwise physical couplings do not justify a pairwise-only residual model**. A threshold on shared occupancy can produce a third-order interaction. For example,

$$
v(S)=\mathbf 1\{|S|\ge3\}
$$

on three agents has zero singleton and pair effects, but a nonzero triple residual.

There is no algebraic non-identifiability once \(F\), the anchor, and the proposed actions are fixed. There can nevertheless be non-identifiability from **incomplete observations** or **insufficient encoded features**. Also, anchoring does not make the residual mean-zero or orthogonal under your training distribution; anchored and distribution-based ANOVA decompositions are distinct. ([UNSW Sites][2])

### 1.2 Separate numerical cancellation, stochastic noise, and missing information

**Exact deterministic labels do not acquire stochastic variance merely because they involve subtraction.** Three different issues need different remedies.

**Numerical precision.** Small differences can be unreliable when the large counterfactual values already contain rounding error. Audit float64 **inside the evaluator**, not just when subtracting its outputs. Casting an already-rounded float32 value to float64 cannot recover lost information. Where possible, cancel unchanged physical contributions before summing the contrast, and use higher-precision spot checks to verify that the interaction is resolved. The relevant numerical distinction is between cancellation itself and errors already present in its operands. ([Oracle Docs][3])

**Noisy counterfactual estimates.** For \(k=|A|\),

$$
\Psi(A)=v(A)-\sum_{i\in A}v(\{i\})+(k-1)v(\varnothing).
$$

If the counterfactual-error covariance matrix is \(\Sigma\), the residual-error variance is

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\Psi-\Psi)=c^\top\Sigma c,
\quad c=(1,-1,\ldots,-1,k-1).
$$

For independent equal-variance errors on the distinct evaluations, with one reused baseline estimate, this becomes

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\Psi-\Psi)
=(k^2-k+2)\sigma^2.
$$

That calculation **does not apply to exact deterministic evaluations**. Paired simulations or common random numbers can reduce contrast variance through favorable covariance, but sharing a seed is not a universal guarantee. ([PubsOnLine][4])

**Feature-induced uncertainty.** Several different physical states can collapse to the same encoded input while having different deterministic residuals. The learner then sees irreducible ambiguity, even though the simulator is noiseless. This is your most important live concern.

A further complication arises when singleton effects are learned. If

$$
\widehat d_i=d_i+\epsilon_i,
$$

then a label formed by subtracting \(\widehat d_i\) is

$$
v(A)-v(\varnothing)-\sum_i\widehat d_i
=\Psi(A)-\sum_i\epsilon_i.
$$

That head is learning **interaction plus singleton-error correction**, not the original interaction. This may be intentional, but it changes the estimand. For an interaction-identification experiment, use exact singleton differences.

### 1.3 Learning \(F\) directly is not automatically better

Suppose the baseline and singleton effects are exact and fixed, and define

$$
\widehat v(A)=v(\varnothing)+\sum_{i\in A}d_i+\widehat\Psi(A).
$$

Then

$$
(\widehat v(A)-v(A))^2
=(\widehat\Psi(A)-\Psi(A))^2.
$$

**Calling this a full-value model changes nothing about its squared-error training objective.**

An unrestricted full-value model is different. It may benefit from additional raw-value observations or a useful shared physical representation. But high accuracy on \(F\) does not guarantee accurate interactions. If its errors satisfy \(|e(S)|\le\epsilon\) on every required counterfactual, then, for \(k\ge2\),

$$
|\widehat\Psi(A)-\Psi(A)|\le 2k\epsilon.
$$

This is a worst-case bound, not inevitable amplification. Any **modular error**

$$
e(S)=b+\sum_{i\in S}u_i
$$

cancels exactly in the residual. What matters is the nonadditive structure of the prediction errors, not only their overall magnitude.

**My recommendation:** retain direct contrast supervision. Consider a shared full-value model when additional \(F\)-labels or physical intermediate targets genuinely provide extra information—not merely because “residuals seem difficult.” Consider explicit Möbius parameterization only with a validated order, sparsity, or sharing assumption.

## 2. Feature sufficiency: an exact criterion and a constructive counterexample

### 2.1 The criterion you are looking for exists

Let \(X\) be the full physical input, and let \(T(X)\) be the **complete representation actually available to the predictor**, including \(Z\) and every auxiliary input branch.

Then

$$
\boxed{
\exists q:\Psi(X)=q(T(X))
\iff
T(x)=T(x')\Longrightarrow\Psi(x)=\Psi(x')
\quad\text{for all feasible }x,x'.
}
$$

The proof is elementary. A deterministic predictor must give the same answer to identical inputs. Conversely, if the residual is constant over every group of inputs sharing the same representation, define \(q\) to return that common value.

This is a test of **information sufficiency**. It does not guarantee that a particular network can learn \(q\) efficiently.

### 2.2 Same scalar sum, opposite interaction signs

Here is a designed collision.

Take three binary agents, anchored at zero, and let all three propose moving to one. Define a pair-additive value

$$
F_W(x)=\sum_{i<j}(W_{ij}^{2}-1)x_ix_j.
$$

Every singleton value and the baseline are zero. Compare:

| Case | \((W_{12},W_{13},W_{23})\) | Sum of couplings | Exact \(\Psi(\{1,2,3\})\) |
| ---- | -------------------------- | ---------------: | ------------------------: |
| P    | \((2,0,0)\)                |                2 |                    \(+1\) |
| Q    | \((1,1,0)\)                |                2 |                    \(-1\) |

Coalition size, actions, singleton effects, and scalar coupling sum are identical. The interaction changes sign.

**No model receiving only that sum, with all other encoded inputs equal, can predict both correctly.** This failure requires neither high-order interactions nor a complicated graph: it occurs in a purely pair-additive value.

There are two important qualifications.

First, this is a counterexample for the class of possible targets, **not proof that your actual evaluator has this collision**. Another part of \(Z\) might already encode the coupling pattern.

Second, a sum can be sufficient. If the target really is

$$
\Psi(A)=\sum_{i<j\in A}W_{ij},
$$

then that scalar is exactly the required information. Your audit must determine whether the summed quantities were **raw couplings, transformed pair effects, exact interaction contributions, or inputs that still require resource-dependent processing**.

Even an unordered collection of all edge weights can be insufficient when the target depends on **which endpoints share which edges or resources**. Preserve incidence and direction, not merely a histogram of relation values.

### 2.3 The resulting error floor is quantifiable

For squared error,

$$
\inf_q\mathbb E[(\Psi-q(T))^2]
=\mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(\Psi\mid T)].
$$

This follows by expanding around \(\mathbb E[\Psi\mid T]\).

For two equally likely indistinguishable inputs whose residuals differ by \(\Delta\), the minimum average squared error is

$$
\frac{\Delta^2}{4}.
$$

In the example above, it is \(1\). More training cannot beat that floor with those features.

### 2.4 How to make the test operational

**Prove factorization through the evaluator.** Trace the quantities needed to calculate the contrast: endpoint-specific couplings, resource states, nonlinear transformations, thresholds, and relevant nonmember contributions. Retain those arguments or prove that discarded information cancels from \(\Psi\).

**Search for feasible collisions.** Redistribute coupling mass while preserving its sum, swap edge endpoints while preserving aggregate statistics, or redistribute demand across resources while preserving total demand. Hold the **entire encoded representation** fixed and compare exact residuals.

**Use small exhaustive cases.** Four to eight agents are enough to expose many aggregation failures and higher-order effects. A found collision is decisive. Failure to find one is not a general sufficiency proof unless the search exhausts the relevant domain.

This is the constructive alternative to waiting for months of failed training.

## 3. Which architectures fit this problem?

### 3.1 The Deep Sets premise needs correction

A nonlinear Deep Sets model is

$$
f(A)=\rho\!\left(\sum_{i\in A}\phi(x_i)\right),
$$

not simply \(\sum_i f_i(x_i)\).

For example,

$$
\sum_{i<j}x_ix_j
=
\frac12\left[
\left(\sum_i x_i\right)^2-\sum_i x_i^2
\right].
$$

Taking \(\phi(x)=(x,x^2)\) represents this pairwise interaction exactly.

Deep Sets have universality results under specified assumptions. Their latent dimension can still impose severe worst-case limitations; Wagstaff and colleagues show that insufficient latent dimension can make some targets no better approximable than by a constant baseline. ([NIPS 论文集][5])

The relevant distinction is:

> **A learned multidimensional set representation is not equivalent to a fixed scalar aggregation performed before learning.**

Neither a Set Transformer nor a graph network can recover arbitrary external relations absent from its inputs.

### 3.2 Start with explicit signed pair factors

A useful baseline is

$$
\widehat\Psi_2(A)=
\sum_{i<j\in A}
h_\theta(z_i,a_i^0,a_i,z_j,a_j^0,a_j,e_{ij},Z).
$$

This naturally gives zero for empty sets and singletons and permits positive and negative interactions.

For these terms to represent actual pair-order coefficients, their context may include the fixed anchor, but they should not change with the rest of the selected coalition. If the pair factors receive messages from all selected members, they can encode higher-order effects—but their outputs are no longer identifiable pair Möbius coefficients.

**Relevant evidence:** Relation Networks explicitly model relations, while **Deep Coordination Graphs** are especially close to your multi-agent setting: they factor joint action values into pair payoffs and use parameter sharing and low-rank approximations. Their demonstrations support this structural approach, but do not establish success for your particular residual-learning regime. ([NeurIPS Proceedings][6])

For 100 agents, a fixed proposal vector has 4,950 unordered pairs. Multiple candidate coalitions and action alternatives multiply the cost, making shared or low-rank parameterizations relevant.

### 3.3 Shared-resource nonlinearities deserve their own representation

A pair-only model can be wrong even with perfect edge features. An agent–resource graph or factor graph may be a better match.

For example, where resource load changes add across members, an anchored resource model can be written

$$
\widehat\Psi_{\mathrm{res}}(A)=
\sum_r\left[
g_r\!\left(\ell_r^0+\sum_{i\in A}\delta\ell_{ir}\right)
-g_r(\ell_r^0)
-\sum_{i\in A}
\left\{g_r(\ell_r^0+\delta\ell_{ir})-g_r(\ell_r^0)\right\}
\right].
$$

This construction has exact empty/singleton zeros and can represent higher-order effects through nonlinear \(g_r\).

This is **my proposed parameterization**, not a universal reduction of resource allocation to scalar loads. It is appropriate only when those resource variables are sufficient and the additive-load assumption is valid.

Before choosing pair-only versus resource-aware modeling, measure

$$
R_{\ge3}(A)=
\Psi(A)-\sum_{i<j\in A}\Psi(\{i,j\}).
$$

If this remainder matters for operational decisions, a genuinely degree-two model is misspecified.

### 3.4 Transformers and graph networks are alternatives, not automatic repairs

Set Transformers introduce attention-based interaction modeling. Their original experiments support attention on some relational tasks, but not a universal advantage: simple max pooling performed better in the reported maximum-regression comparison. ([Proceedings of Machine Learning Research][7])

For your task, attention should receive pair attributes through edge-aware attention, edge tokens, or an appropriate graph representation.

Graph networks have limits too. Standard message-passing families can fail to distinguish structures beyond their Weisfeiler–Leman discrimination power. With identical initial node features, a six-cycle and two disjoint triangles can remain indistinguishable to ordinary message passing, although a triangle-dependent target differs. ([arXiv][8])

Also, accepting variable-size inputs is not a guarantee of generalization to larger sets. Published work identifies size-generalization failures associated with shifts in local graph structure. ([Proceedings of Machine Learning Research][9])

**Architecture judgment:** start with the smallest signed, edge/resource-aware model justified by the evaluator. Use an edge-aware transformer when measured context dependence requires its flexibility. I found no controlled primary study establishing a winner for your exact approximately 100-agent, few-coalition-labels-per-anchor setting.

## 4. How can you obtain more signal per evaluation?

### 4.1 First audit the “four counterfactuals” statement

For \(k\ge2\), your stated residual needs

$$
v(\varnothing),\quad
\{v(\{i\})\}_{i\in A},\quad
v(A):
$$

**\(k+2\) distinct values before reuse.** Four suffice for a pair. With cached baseline and singleton values, another coalition may require just **one new evaluation**.

Four evaluations can instead calculate a two-block interaction:

$$
D(B,C)=v(B\cup C)-v(B)-v(C)+v(\varnothing),
$$

for disjoint \(B,C\). But

$$
\boxed{
D(B,C)=\Psi(B\cup C)-\Psi(B)-\Psi(C).
}
$$

That is not the full residual of \(B\cup C\) unless internal block interactions vanish or are added back.

**This is an implementation question to resolve, not enough evidence to declare your labels wrong.** Existing caches could explain the four-call accounting.

### 4.2 Overlapping subsets are useful—but not independent new information

Fix proposed actions for a small block and evaluate all its subset masks, with nonmembers at the anchor.

For six agents, **64 raw values** yield:

* **57 nontrivial residual labels**, \(2^6-1-6\);
* every Möbius coefficient within that block;
* **240 conditional pair contrasts**, \(\binom62 2^4\).

The conditional contrast satisfies

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta_{ij}v(S)
&=v(S\cup\{i,j\})-v(S\cup\{i\})\\
&\quad-v(S\cup\{j\})+v(S)\\
&=\sum_{U\subseteq S}m(U\cup\{i,j\}).
\end{aligned}
$$

These contrasts expose how pair effects change with context and higher-order interactions.

However, **they still originate from only 64 scalar observations**. Do not treat the derived contrasts as hundreds of independent measurements.

Retaining per-agent or per-resource outputs already computed by the evaluator can also provide extra supervision without additional full evaluations, where those outputs correspond to meaningful components.

### 4.3 One coalition value constrains many coefficients, not each subset separately

A labeled coalition supplies

$$
\Psi(A_t)=
\sum_{\substack{T\subseteq A_t\\|T|\ge2}}m(T).
$$

It constrains one sum. It does not reveal every term in that sum.

For an exact pairwise model,

$$
y=X\theta,
\qquad
X_{t,ij}=\mathbf1\{i,j\in A_t\}.
$$

There are 4,950 unrestricted pair coefficients at a fixed 100-agent anchor/proposal combination. Dense identification requires adequate design rank. Repeated grand-coalition observations give the same row pattern and cannot distinguish those coefficients.

Under exact pair-additivity,

$$
1+100+\binom{100}{2}=5{,}051
$$

baseline, singleton, and pair values determine the entire Boolean-mask game. Fewer observations require justified sharing, sparsity, low rank, or another restriction.

**More anchors help only through structure shared between anchors.** They do not substitute for within-anchor intervention coverage when each anchor’s game is otherwise arbitrary.

### 4.4 Sparse Möbius learning is an established alternative—with assumptions

Kang and colleagues’ NeurIPS 2024 paper, *Learning to Understand: Identifying Interactions via the Möbius Transform*, provides recovery algorithms with bounds including \(O(Kn)\) and, in a low-degree regime, \(O(Kt\log n)\), for \(K\) nonzero coefficients and maximum degree \(t\). These require specific support-distribution, sparsity, coefficient, and asymptotic assumptions. They are not worst-case guarantees for every approximately sparse physical system. ([NeurIPS Proceedings][10])

This is relevant when your measured interaction structure and query access match the recovery assumptions. It is not a reason to parameterize all \(2^{100}\) coefficients.

SHAP-IQ and SVARM-IQ illustrate efficient reuse of values for interaction estimation, but **Shapley-style interactions average across contexts**. They are not interchangeable with your fixed-anchor residual. ([arXiv][11])

### 4.5 Collect variation the selector actually needs

My recommended sampling change is **more comparisons among coalitions at the same anchor and similar size**, including overlapping subsets and resource-boundary cases—not simply more unrelated anchors.

Assess performance against both a zero-residual baseline and a size-only baseline. Measure within-anchor, within-size ranking and realized selection gain. A pooled correlation can look good because it captures differences between sizes while failing to distinguish the coalitions the coordinator needs to compare.

Normalization can help training, but invert it before assembling the deployment score. Dividing the deployed interaction by coalition size changes the objective; it is not merely a numerical fix.

## 5. What does “always select all agents” diagnose?

### 5.1 First establish whether it is actually wrong

For total improvement, with an optional execution cost,

$$
G(A)=\sum_{i\in A}d_i+\Psi(A)-c(A).
$$

Maximizing \(\Psi(A)\) alone is a different objective.

Also,

$$
\exists A\text{ small}:G(A)>0
$$

does **not** imply

$$
G(A)>G(N).
$$

A profitable small coalition can coexist with a still-better grand coalition. To establish the reported failure, you need a small feasible coalition that **strictly beats the grand coalition under the intended score**, or a genuine size preference/budget in the objective.

### 5.2 A size-correlated scalar is a plausible explanation, not sufficient evidence

The following conditions would be sufficient: adding members makes the aggregate nondecreasing; the learned score is nondecreasing in that aggregate; no other score term offsets it; and the grand coalition is feasible.

But a scalar sum alone does not establish those conditions. The network can learn a decreasing or nonmonotone function. Several independent mechanisms can also cause your symptom.

**An undertrained or nearly zero residual head.** If

$$
\widehat\Psi(A)\approx0,
$$

then a total-value selector approximately maximizes \(\sum_i d_i-c(A)\). With positive singleton gains and no offsetting constraints or costs, all-agents is the expected choice. For an interaction-only selector, an exactly zero head instead makes tie-breaking decisive.

**Small bias accumulated across many pairs.** An upward bias \(b\) per pair contributes

$$
b\binom{k}{2}.
$$

A bias of \(0.01\) per pair is \(49.5\) at size 100, versus \(0.01\) at size two. Exact empty/singleton zeros do not prevent this. A generic head multiplied by \(k(k-1)\) can create analogous scale sensitivity.

**Selection or implementation rules.** Append-only search without a stop option, omitted small candidates, tie-breaking, wrong masks, missing costs, inverse-normalization errors, or clipping signed residuals to nonnegative values can all favor large coalitions.

**Optimization exploiting prediction errors.** Selecting the largest estimated score can favor optimistic errors, and surrogate optimization can move into poorly supported inputs. The optimizer’s curse and offline model-based optimization literature document these related problems. Neither phenomenon alone predicts that the selected set must specifically be the grand coalition. ([PubsOnLine][12])

### 5.3 The most discriminating tests

| Test                                                                                                                                    | What it separates                                                                                                                              |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Exactly score a fixed candidate panel**, including empty, known-good small, intermediate, and grand coalitions; run the same selector | If a strictly inferior grand coalition still wins, the problem is score assembly, search, feasibility, masking, or ties—not residual learning. |
| **Replace only \(\widehat\Psi\) with zero**                                                                                             | If behavior remains all-agents, the learned interaction is unnecessary to explain the symptom. Inspect singleton gains and selection defaults. |
| **Log \(\widehat\Psi\), true \(\Psi\), singleton sum, cost, and final score separately by size and checkpoint**                         | Distinguishes early-epoch shrinkage, accumulating bias, unit errors, and a legitimate size preference.                                         |
| **Compare size-only, scalar-aggregate, explicit-pair, and resource-aware models on matched within-anchor/within-size cases**            | Tests whether the model learns relationships rather than cardinality.                                                                          |
| **Test candidate order, no-op members, permutation consistency, and head-scale sensitivity**                                            | Exposes tie-breaking, mask errors, cardinality artifacts, and calibration dependence.                                                          |

For nested growth, the relevant quantity is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\widehat G(A\cup\{i\})-\widehat G(A)
&=d_i+\widehat\Psi(A\cup\{i\})-\widehat\Psi(A)\\
&\quad-[c(A\cup\{i\})-c(A)].
\end{aligned}
$$

Compare this with the exact marginal. **The sign of the residual itself is not the same question as whether adding another member is beneficial.**

A particularly useful invariant: adding a member whose proposed action equals its anchor action must not change the physical residual. An explicit membership cost can change the total score, but not that counterfactual identity.

## 6. The decision before further training

**I would not change the estimand yet. I would change the diagnostic order.**

First, resolve the label accounting, exact-versus-learned singleton convention, numerical precision, and oracle-scored selection test. These can reject an implementation without training anything.

Next, produce either a feasible collision for the actual scalar encoding or a factorization argument showing that it is sufficient. Preserve endpoint-specific and resource-specific information where the evaluator needs it.

Then measure the higher-order remainder using cached singleton values and overlapping small subsets. Use that result to choose between a signed pair-factor model and a resource-aware extension. Judge them on within-anchor selection quality, not only regression fit.

The **established result** is that your residual is a legitimate anchored set-function quantity. The **provable impossibility** is learning it exactly from features that identify physically distinct cases with different residuals. The **unresolved application-specific questions** are whether your actual encoder has such collisions, how much higher-order structure matters, and which sufficient architecture generalizes under your label budget. The literature does not settle those last questions for your system, but they can be tested far more directly than another long training run.

**Bottom line: keep the residual, repair or justify the information supplied to it, and separate prediction failure from selection failure. Switching to \(F\), Möbius coefficients, or a transformer before those checks risks preserving the same defect under a new formulation.**
