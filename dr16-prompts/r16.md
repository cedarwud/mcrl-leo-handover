## Recommendation

**Use a resource-aware, threshold-explicit model—not an unrestricted collection of third-, fourth-, and higher-order agent interactions. But before fitting it, replay selection using exact pairwise interactions.** That replay can establish whether omitting higher-order structure actually costs you useful decisions on the existing corpus.

Your proposed anchored resource residual is the right starting family **provided that resource load, together with its supplied context, is sufficient to determine the relevant response**. It is not automatically sufficient merely because the dominant mechanism involves occupancy.

Also, these are not three mutually exclusive choices: a resource-aware form specifies **what the model can represent**; a ranking objective specifies **how it is trained**; accepting bounded misspecification specifies **what error you are willing to tolerate**.

The important distinction is:

> Your measurement establishes failure of the exact, pair-calibrated value reconstruction. It does not establish a 38.5% ranking error, nor a lower bound on selection regret for every possible refitted pairwise scorer.

Below, “derivation” denotes a mathematical consequence of your setup; recommendations about what to implement are my judgement.

## 1. What your measurement proves—and what it does not

Write the exact second-order anchored reconstruction as

$$
Q_2(A)=F(a_0)+\sum_{i\in A}d_i
+\sum_{\{i,j\}\subseteq A}\Psi(\{i,j\}).
$$

Then your measurement is exactly

$$
F(a_A)-Q_2(A)=R_{\ge3}(A).
$$

For triples, this is the pure third-order anchored interaction. For larger coalitions, it contains all orders from three through the coalition size. This follows from the unique multilinear representation of a finite set function; the quadratic representation retains only terms involving at most two selection variables. ([arXiv][1])

Thus, accepting your evaluations and their common normalization:

**The unique reconstruction that matches the baseline, singletons, and exact pairs is wrong by more than \(10^{-3}\) on 38.5% of your tested coalitions of size at least three.**

No quadratic can reproduce those coalitions **and their lower-order calibration values simultaneously**. A refitted quadratic could redistribute the error onto the pair values, however. Consequently, 38.5% is not an unavoidable error rate for a refitted model, and it is not a fraction of variance or of decision quality lost.

One terminology correction matters for subsequent analysis: call the quantity \(R_{\ge3}\), not “third-order share,” except when reporting the size-three stratum. Your six size-six observations do not, by themselves, demonstrate a sixth-order effect.

All of this also assumes a genuinely pairwise scorer: each pair coefficient may depend on the fixed anchor and the two proposed actions, but not on the other coalition members. A “pair coefficient” conditioned on the coalition’s resulting occupancy can already contain higher-order structure.

## 2. The model family: resource-cardinality responses, with explicit context

### Why your proposed family fits the mechanism

An arbitrary function of the number of active variables is an established **cardinality potential**: a compact higher-order factor whose input is a count rather than a list of separately parameterized interactions. Your signed arrivals/departures formulation is the corresponding anchored resource-load construction. The cardinality-potential literature explicitly allows arbitrary count-response functions, not only smooth or concave ones. ([智能概率系統實驗室][2])

Define the anchored residual operator

$$
\mathcal D_A H
=
H(A)-H(\varnothing)
-\sum_{i\in A}\bigl[H(\{i\})-H(\varnothing)\bigr].
$$

With

$$
n_r(A)=n_{r0}+\sum_{i\in A}\Delta n_{ir},
$$

your proposal is

$$
\widehat\Psi(A)
=
\mathcal D_A\left[\sum_r g_r(n_r(A))\right].
$$

**Derivation:** this is exact whenever the objective is a sum of these resource functions plus terms additive in individual moves. The latter disappear under \(\mathcal D_A\).

The useful change in modelling perspective is that **one resource threshold generates many interaction coefficients, but you need not learn those coefficients separately**.

For example, let \(g(n)=G\,\mathbf1[n\ge3]\).

At baseline occupancy zero, three arrivals yield a third-order interaction \(G\): all singleton and pair effects are zero.

At baseline occupancy one, every pair of arrivals activates the resource and has interaction \(G\). Three arrivals still produce only one activation, so

$$
R_{\ge3}(\{i,j,k\})=G-3G=-2G.
$$

The larger-coalition correction is accounting for one shared event, not discovering three independent affinities. This also explains why the mechanism can produce either sign.

### The form I would actually start with

My preferred first candidate is

$$
\widehat F(A)
=
F(a_0)+\sum_{i\in A}d_i
+\mathcal D_A \widehat H,
$$

where

$$
\widehat H(A)
=
H_{\mathrm{known}}(A)
+
\sum_r
g_{\theta,\mathrm{type}(r)}
\bigl(n_r(A),c_{r0}\bigr).
$$

Here, \(c_{r0}\) is explicit anchor context, and parameters are shared across comparable resource types rather than fitting an unrelated curve for every physical beam or anchor.

I would put **known, inexpensive switching/accounting rules into \(H_{\mathrm{known}}\)**. For instance, where your simulator’s rule really is a fixed cost while nonempty, its objective contribution can be represented directly by a term proportional to

$$
-\mathbf1[n_r(A)>0].
$$

That captures the last-occupant effect at every coalition size. There is little reason to ask a learner to rediscover the conjunction “all original occupants have left” when final occupancy is available exactly.

Likewise, retain a known mode-selection gate explicitly wherever it can be evaluated cheaply from available inputs. Learn uncertain amplitudes or expensive continuous responses around it. Keep “no transmitted mode” distinct from “chain switched off” when your evaluator distinguishes them.

### Three assumptions to test

**First: load sufficiency.** Can two candidate assignments with the same proposed resource-load representation nevertheless have different responses because different users moved?

The relevant test is not merely “same final occupancy.” Your residual model uses the baseline, joint, and singleton resource states. Look for collisions in that **complete supplied representation**, with different exact residuals. Such a collision disproves feature sufficiency regardless of the learner.

Context may need to preserve heterogeneous rate requirements, link conditions, or other quantities actually used by your evaluator. Adding a count does not justify discarding them.

**Second: resource separability.** Additive accounting does not imply independent resource responses.

A total may be a sum of beam contributions while each beam contribution depends on other beams’ changing interference or shared constraints. A function \(g_r(n_r,c_{r0})\) with frozen context cannot express those candidate-dependent changes.

A direct test is to construct move groups \(U,V\) affecting disjoint **modelled factor scopes**, and compute

$$
F(U\cup V)-F(U)-F(V)+F(\varnothing).
$$

Your separable model predicts zero. A material nonzero value indicates a missing shared factor or coupling. Include any satellite-level shared factor when determining whether the scopes are genuinely disjoint.

If this test fails, my next extension would be a response conditioned on the affected resource neighbourhood—not a generic tensor of agent triples. Preserve the identities and couplings that distinguish the counterexample.

**Third: objective separability.** For \(B-\lambda E\), with one fixed \(\lambda\), resource contributions can add if the underlying accounting supports it. For \(B/E\),

$$
\frac{\sum_r B_r}{\sum_r E_r}
\ne
\sum_r g_r(n_r)
$$

in general. In that case, model bits and energy separately, compose the ratio, and only then take anchored differences. A nonlinear objective transformation can itself introduce interactions.

**My judgement:** your mechanism strongly favours this resource-aware family, but these tests determine whether **occupancy-only**, **contextual occupancy**, or **coupled-resource** functions are warranted.

## 3. Learning the staircase without smoothing away the mechanism

### Use a discrete response representation first

For integer occupancy, a lookup table over the relevant range is a natural starting point:

$$
g(n)=\theta_n,\qquad n=0,\ldots,N.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
g(n)=\alpha+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\beta_k\,\mathbf1[n\ge k].
$$

Every discrete staircase is exactly representable. Unknown threshold locations correspond to nonzero jump coefficients \(\beta_k\); they do not require differentiating through a learned hard threshold.

This is not a claim that neural networks cannot represent your response. On a finite integer grid, even a continuous piecewise-linear function can interpolate arbitrary values. The concern is the inductive bias and the training signal—not an inherent impossibility of representing integer steps.

For context-dependent responses, I would retain the hard occupancy or known mode indicators and learn their **context-dependent amplitudes**, using a small shared model. That makes the threshold visible in the architecture.

### Aggregate labels give you a concrete inverse problem

You need not assign a fictional training label to each beam.

For illustration, suppose resources share one response curve with step basis

$$
\phi_k(n)=\mathbf1[n\ge k].
$$

Construct a design row for each labelled coalition:

$$
X_{A,k}
=
\sum_r
\left[
\phi_k(n_r(A))
-\phi_k(n_{r0})
-\sum_{i\in A}
\left(
\phi_k(n_r(\{i\}))-\phi_k(n_{r0})
\right)
\right].
$$

After subtracting any known residual contribution, fit

$$
y_A=\Psi(A)-\mathcal D_AH_{\mathrm{known}}
\approx\sum_kX_{A,k}\beta_k.
$$

This is linear regression in the unknown jump amplitudes, although it is higher-order and nonlinear in coalition membership. Separate blocks can represent resource types or prespecified contextual regimes.

**This is the first model I would fit:** it makes support, rank deficiency, and failure of the proposed structure inspectable before introducing a large neural model.

### Regularize jumps, not away from jumps

An established approach to ordered coefficients is fused-lasso/total-variation regularization: penalizing absolute differences between adjacent response levels encourages piecewise-constant profiles. Zero-order trend filtering is the corresponding piecewise-constant formulation. These methods explicitly permit jumps rather than requiring a globally smooth curve. ([OUP Academic][3])

Applied here, a candidate objective is

$$
\min_{\theta}
\|X_{\mathrm{table}}\theta-y\|_2^2
+
\lambda\sum_n|\theta_{n+1}-\theta_n|.
$$

However, **this penalty can still shrink or erase a rare jump**. My implementation preference would be to leave physically known transitions unpenalized or hard-coded, use regularization primarily for unknown structure, and evaluate jump contrasts explicitly rather than selecting \(\lambda\) solely by pooled squared error.

Because your labels are exact, a substantial unexplained jump should first trigger an examination of missing context or inappropriate parameter sharing—not an assumption that the observation is noise.

Also, do not impose monotonicity, convexity, or concavity on the final bits-minus-energy response merely because individual components have such a property. A known signed activation cost can be constrained separately.

### The identifiability trap

**Derivation:** for additive loads,

$$
\mathcal D_A(a+bn_r)=0.
$$

Therefore, residual-only labels cannot identify an affine component of \(g_r\). Multiple resources can also be confounded when their loads always change together.

Consequently, fitting aggregate residuals may identify useful **interaction predictions** without uniquely identifying the physical resource curves.

Inspect the design matrix’s rank and null space after accounting for these unavoidable ambiguities. Fix a stated convention for unidentified components, or add raw objective/component measurements. If the evaluator already exposes per-resource bits, power, or mode states, recording those outputs may improve identification without requiring another physics solve.

**A regularizer selects among indistinguishable explanations; it does not turn an unidentifiable curve into a physically identified one.**

## 4. Does your size distribution support this?

Your supplied counts imply that sizes four through six contribute **312 observations, about 2.62% of the corpus**. Sizes five and six contribute only 42.

That supports different conclusions for different model families.

### Arbitrary high-order coefficients: not a credible direction

I would not recommend learning freely varying fourth-, fifth-, and sixth-order agent/action interactions from those strata. The counts alone cannot support that degree of freedom, particularly across changing anchors and actions.

Nor should the six size-six cases be used to claim general selection reliability for size six. They are useful counterexamples or diagnostic cases, not broad validation.

### A shared low-dimensional resource curve: potentially feasible

Coalition order and response-function sample complexity are different.

For two identical arrivals at a resource with baseline occupancy \(n\),

$$
\Psi_2(n)=g(n+2)-2g(n+1)+g(n).
$$

Thus, pair observations across successive baseline occupancies reveal second differences of \(g\). If these differences cover the relevant range and the resource response is otherwise isolated, they determine \(g\) up to an affine component. Higher-order effects then follow from the same curve.

**A model can therefore learn a six-member threshold effect without needing a separate sixth-order parameter or many size-six training cases.**

But that works only when the resource levels and contexts required by those coalitions are supported. Small-coalition examples at varied anchors may provide that coverage; small-coalition examples concentrated far from the relevant threshold do not.

The coverage question I would ask is:

> How many distinct anchors expose each important resource transition, in each relevant context, and are the resulting aggregate design columns distinguishable?

“11,924 labels” does not answer that question.

### Generate threshold coverage, not just larger sets

My preferred additional evaluations would compare threshold-crossing coalitions with matched non-crossing controls, complete evacuation with “one occupant remains,” and moves with comparable count changes but different relevant context. Larger coalitions are valuable when they reach states or combinations not otherwise exercised.

For selected four-to-six-move cases, evaluate the complete subset closure. A six-move closure contains only 64 subset states before using cached evaluations. This directly separates individual orders and tests whether one resource response explains all subcoalitions consistently.

Finally, split validation by anchor—and by trajectory or scenario if anchors share those dependencies. Do not randomly distribute coalitions from one anchor between training and test and call that new-anchor generalization. Blocking follows the dependence structure rather than treating every row as independent; simulation studies of structured-data validation demonstrate why this distinction matters. ([Nordic Society Oikos][4])

**My judgement:** your corpus justifies a small resource-response experiment. It does not establish sufficient coverage for that experiment to succeed, or support unrestricted order-specific learning.

## 5. Ranking: misspecification matters only where it changes consequential comparisons

For candidates \(A,B\) at one anchor,

$$
F(A)-F(B)
=
Q_2(A)-Q_2(B)
+
R_{\ge3}(A)-R_{\ge3}(B).
$$

If \(Q_2(A)>Q_2(B)\), the ordering reverses exactly when

$$
R_{\ge3}(B)-R_{\ge3}(A)
>
Q_2(A)-Q_2(B).
$$

The relevant quantity is therefore **the difference in omitted residuals relative to the comparison margin**, not the fraction of individually nonzero residuals.

Large residuals can leave ordering unchanged when they affect the contenders similarly. Small residuals can reverse a near tie. Your reported marginal distribution contains neither the required comparison gaps nor the joint placement of the residuals.

So, **yes: the misspecification argument weakens substantially for ranking. The 38.5% result does not prove ordering failure.**

### Train the ordering you actually need

If the selector chooses by total objective improvement, train rankings of

$$
s(A)=\sum_{i\in A}d_i+\widehat\Psi(A),
$$

not rankings of \(\widehat\Psi(A)\) alone. A coalition can have strong positive interaction yet poor total value.

An established option is a within-anchor pair-comparison loss such as

$$
L_{\mathrm{rank}}
=
\sum_{(A,B)}
w_{AB}
\log\!\left[
1+\exp\!\left(
-y_{AB}\frac{s(A)-s(B)}{T}
\right)
\right],
$$

where \(y_{AB}\) is the true ordering. RankNet introduced this logistic comparison approach; its grouping by query corresponds here to grouping by anchor. “Pairwise ranking loss” means comparing two candidate coalitions—it does **not** restrict their internal interaction order. ([ICML][5])

My preference would be to emphasize plausible top contenders, ignore practically immaterial near-ties, and prevent anchors with many sampled candidates from dominating. Retain calibration supervision if scores also control an absolute improvement gate or are interpreted as physical quantities.

Decision-focused learning provides established evidence that reducing prediction error and improving downstream decisions are different objectives, including under model misspecification. It does not guarantee that a particular ranking loss will solve your coalition problem. ([arXiv][6])

## 6. The cheap diagnostic I would run before training anything new

### A. Exact-pair selection replay

For each anchor \(t\), freeze its labelled feasible candidate pool \(\mathcal C_t\), including the no-move baseline when allowed.

Compute

$$
A_t^*=\arg\max_{A\in\mathcal C_t}F_t(A),
\qquad
A_t^{(2)}=\arg\max_{A\in\mathcal C_t}Q_{2,t}(A),
$$

and

$$
\operatorname{Regret}^{(2)}_t
=
F_t(A_t^*)-F_t(A_t^{(2)}).
$$

Use the actual tie-breaking rule and record important ties separately.

You already computed the pair sums needed for \(R_{\ge3}\). Provided those values and the exact coalition labels are retained, this requires **no alternative model and no new physics evaluations**.

The essential report is the distribution of per-anchor selection regret, the fraction exceeding a prespecified tolerable loss, and the true-value gap after exact reranking of the top \(K\) candidates under \(Q_2\). The last quantity matters if the deployed pipeline can afford a small exact shortlist evaluation.

Interpretation:

| Result                                               | What it establishes                                                                                                                     |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Exact-pair replay selects well                       | Omitting higher orders is not materially limiting selection on these candidate pools. Learning the pair score accurately may be enough. |
| Exact-pair replay misses valuable candidates         | Omitting higher orders causes decision loss for the pair-calibrated reconstruction.                                                     |
| Top-\(K\) exact reranking recovers nearly everything | A pairwise proposal score may remain useful even when its top-one decision is inadequate.                                               |

A high global correlation is not a substitute for this replay. One error at the top can determine the selected action while leaving most comparisons correct.

Two limitations are crucial. First, this concerns the **labelled candidate pools**, not the best coalition in the entire action space. Second, it is an oracle for exact pair calibration—not an upper bound on every possible refitted pairwise ranker. A biased pairwise scorer could rank better by deliberately sacrificing pair-value accuracy.

### B. A rigorous bound for the exact-pair reconstruction

There is a simple useful bound, directly from your decomposition.

At a fixed anchor, let \(A^*\) maximize \(F\) and \(\widehat A\) maximize \(Q_2\). Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
F(A^*)-F(\widehat A)
&=
Q_2(A^*)-Q_2(\widehat A)
+R_{\ge3}(A^*)-R_{\ge3}(\widehat A)\\
&\le
\max_A R_{\ge3}(A)-\min_A R_{\ge3}(A).
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus, **within-anchor residual range**, rather than pooled residual magnitude, bounds truncation regret over that candidate pool.

More generally, if a valid bound gives

$$
|F(A)-Q_2(A)|\le\varepsilon
$$

for every admissible candidate, selection regret is at most \(2\varepsilon\). A \(Q_2\) winner whose score gap exceeds \(2\varepsilon\) is certified to remain the true winner.

Your summary does **not** provide such a uniform bound: the positive maximum \(15.18\) is not necessarily the maximum absolute residual, and the fifth percentile is not the minimum. Empirical bounds on the current pool also do not automatically extend to unseen candidates.

### C. Can *any* admissible quadratic preserve the ranking?

This can be tested without fitting the resource alternative, but the word **admissible** matters.

Represent a quadratic candidate score as

$$
q_\theta(A)=s_0(A)+\phi_2(A)^\top\theta,
$$

where \(s_0\) contains fixed known terms and \(\phi_2\) contains the permitted pair features. When proposed destinations vary, features must identify the corresponding user/action combinations, not just user pairs.

For every important strict preference \(F(A)>F(B)\), require

$$
q_\theta(A)-q_\theta(B)\ge\gamma.
$$

Maximize \(\gamma\), with \(0\le\gamma\le1\), subject to the coefficient restrictions being tested. This is a linear program when those restrictions are linear.

A positive optimum proves that this class can strictly preserve the specified comparisons on the corpus. Failure to achieve a positive margin certifies a ranking obstruction for those comparisons and that class, subject to numerical verification.

But unrestricted anchor-specific coefficients can make the test almost vacuous. With roughly 100 fixed binary move variables, a full quadratic has 4,950 pair coefficients, before unaries, against your median of roughly 46 sampled coalitions per anchor. Feasibility can simply reflect interpolation freedom.

Even more fundamentally, an unrestricted linear score can make any chosen subset \(B\) the unique winner:

$$
q_B(A)=|A\cap B|-|A\setminus B|.
$$

Therefore, **there is no useful unavoidable top-one regret bound for an unrestricted, independently chosen scorer at every anchor**. Meaningful restrictions must reflect your intended system: fixed singletons, pair-calibration tolerances, permitted features, or parameter sharing.

For a specified class, a selection-quality bound is possible. Test each candidate \(B\) for feasibility of

$$
q_\theta(B)\ge q_\theta(A)
\quad\text{for all }A\in\mathcal C_t.
$$

The greatest true \(F(B)\) among feasible candidates is an **optimistic upper bound** on selected value, allowing favourable tie-breaking. Its gap below \(F(A_t^*)\) is a lower bound on unavoidable regret for that restricted class. A favourable result still does not establish that a shared learner can generalize to new anchors.

## 7. What would make me stop blaming higher-order structure?

The strongest early exoneration would be:

**Exact-pair replay has acceptably small regret on representative held-out anchors, including threshold-event cases and competitive larger coalitions, while the learned model performs substantially worse.**

That would direct attention toward feature sufficiency, estimation, training objectives, or candidate generation—not toward adding interaction order.

Conversely, if exact-pair replay repeatedly discards threshold-crossing winners, especially when shortlist reranking also fails, you have decision-level evidence for replacing the pair-calibrated form. That is much stronger than the 38.5% prevalence statistic.

### My decision from the evidence currently available

**The next model candidate should be a shared, contextual resource-response model with explicit staircase and empty-resource gates.** Start with the aggregate linear step-basis formulation; add coupling only where controlled tests require it. Do not begin by fitting separate high-order agent terms.

**The next computation should nevertheless be exact-pair selection replay.** It is already largely paid for by your \(R_{\ge3}\) calculation and determines whether this replacement addresses an actual selection bottleneck.

Use ranking as an optional objective for either family, not as an argument that misspecification has disappeared. Accept bounded misspecification only after measuring a decision-level bound or a clearly scoped held-out regret distribution. Your current numbers establish a value-model limitation; they do not yet establish that its selection consequences are either tolerable or intolerable.
