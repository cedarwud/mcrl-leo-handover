## Verdict

**Your objection is correct. Raising power to \(p_{\mathrm{nominal}}/q\) creates a genuine fade reserve under the assumptions in your derivation. The stated reason for forbidding it is mathematically mistaken. I recommend amending that rule before the main run.**

**Power margin, MODCOD back-off, and combinations of the two are all legitimate engineering approaches.** The problem is not “margin applied through ACM.” It is your particular composition: first remove essentially all nominal headroom by targeting a mode’s decoding threshold, then require additional headroom while prohibiting power adjustment. That produces a structural lowest-mode failure that ordinary fixed-power ACM does not impose. DVB’s implementation guidance explicitly discusses ACM with fixed beam power; the satellite literature also explicitly derives the \(p_0/q\) power-margin construction. ([ETSI][1])

There is also an important correction to the numerical premise: **the package’s 19.2/9.9 dB values are not the link closure for the figure’s 2,000-km geometry.** Using the supplied figure’s constants, I obtain approximately **5.71 dB at boresight and 2.70 dB at the half-power edge**. This materially changes the answer about higher-order modulation.

The conclusions below distinguish mathematical consequences of your rule, reported package observations, and independent numerical checks. I did not rerun the simulator or reproduce its aggregate availability.

## 1. What engineers actually do with fade margin

### Fixed-power ACM: sacrifice rate, not necessarily add power

A recognized forward-link design assigns RF power to a beam or carrier and adapts each recipient’s coding and modulation to its channel. Favorable conditions permit greater throughput; fading causes a move to more robust MODCODs.

This is not merely an interpretation of the standard’s tables. **ETSI TR 102 376-1, §4.4.1**, describes precisely this approach, explicitly assuming fixed beam-power allocation. Its Annex E also discusses shifted switching thresholds and hysteresis for imperfect channel estimates and adaptation behavior. ([ETSI][1])

The essential distinction from your rule is:

> **Fixed-power ACM chooses a mode for an available power budget. Your controller chooses a power budget just sufficient for one nominal mode, then withdraws that mode’s eligibility.**

Those are different control policies.

### Power control and combined power/rate adaptation are also real

Power can be provisioned above the no-fade requirement to protect a selected mode. Adaptive power control can additionally respond to observed channel changes, subject to headroom and payload constraints.

The DVB-S2X implementation guide explicitly discusses operation using **ACM and/or uplink power control**. As a concrete equipment example, Comtech’s CDM-780 datasheet lists both ACM and automatic uplink power control for its DVB-S2/S2X waveforms. This establishes that power adaptation and ACM are not mutually exclusive practices. ([ETSI][2])

However, **uplink power control is not interchangeable with increasing spacecraft downlink RF power**. For your model, the closer literature is joint ACM and **downlink** power allocation. Angeletti and colleagues explicitly analyze boosting faded beams from a shared payload power pool, combined with ACM, when minimum rate and availability matter. They also explain the resulting interference coupling between co-frequency beams. ([ResearchGate][3])

**There is no universal DVB-mandated split such as “half the margin in power, half in MODCOD.”** The cited implementation guidance presents alternatives rather than one mandatory allocation. The appropriate split depends on available power flexibility, interference, acceptable rate degradation, and the service requirement. ([ETSI][1])

The useful literature terms are **fade mitigation techniques**, **ACM switching margin**, **joint power and rate adaptation**, and—when explicitly formulated probabilistically—**outage-constrained power/rate allocation**.

## 2. Direct answer: the argument against \(p_{\mathrm{nominal}}/q\) is wrong

Let:

$$
\Gamma_m=\text{decoding threshold of the selected transmitted mode},
$$

$$
D=N+I,\qquad
\gamma_{\mathrm{nom}}=\frac{hp}{D},
$$

where \(h\) is nominal wanted-link power gain and, initially, \(D\) is held fixed. Let \(G\) be the **relative power-gain fading multiplier**, so that

$$
\gamma_{\mathrm{real}}=G\frac{hp}{D}.
$$

Nominal exact-threshold power is

$$
p_0=\frac{\Gamma_mD}{h}.
$$

Now choose

$$
p_{\mathrm{reserve}}=\frac{p_0}{q},
\qquad q=F_G^{-1}(0.10).
$$

Then

$$
\gamma_{\mathrm{nom}}=\frac{\Gamma_m}{q},
\qquad
\gamma_{\mathrm{real}}=\Gamma_m\frac{G}{q}.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\gamma_{\mathrm{real}}\geq\Gamma_m
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad G\geq q.
$$

For a continuous, correctly specified fading distribution and its exact quantile,

$$
\Pr(\text{decode})=0.90.
$$

**The nominal link is above threshold. It is the lower-decile faded link that sits at threshold. That is the reserve—not its cancellation.**

The additional margin is

$$
M_f=-10\log_{10}q\ \mathrm{dB}.
$$

This construction has a particularly direct primary-source match: in *Adaptive power and rate control for satellite communications in Ka band*, Stojanovic derives \(P_T=P_{T0}/G_{\mathrm{out}}\) and identifies the reciprocal outage-gain factor as the required fixed fade margin. See printed page 2968, immediately after equation (7). ([Massachusetts Institute of Technology][4])

### What qualifies the 90% statement

**A binding cap changes the result.** With

$$
p=\min\left(p_{\max},\frac{p_0}{q}\right),
$$

the selected mode does not automatically retain 90% reliability. Under the fixed-denominator model, its success probability is

$$
1-F_G\!\left(\frac{\Gamma_mD}{hp}\right).
$$

A cap-induced shortfall must therefore produce an explicit rate-feasibility failure, a lower mode, or another resource decision.

**Interference must be recomputed when other beams change power.** Multiplying an existing multi-beam power vector by \(1/q\) is not generally an exact robust solution. For example, scaling all powers equally gives

$$
\gamma_i'=\frac{S_i}{qN_i+I_i},
$$

not \(\gamma_i/q\). The interference-limited component does not improve under uniform scaling. This is a genuine reason to coordinate or limit power increases; it is not evidence that power margin is fictitious. The multibeam downlink literature explicitly identifies these coupled effects. ([ResearchGate][3])

**The quantile must match the actual uncertainty.** A wanted-link quantile with interference frozen at its nominal value is not automatically a 90% guarantee when interference is also random. Nor is a per-transmission 90% guarantee equivalent to 90% aggregate service availability or uninterrupted service throughout an entire multi-interval decision.

Finally, **a genuine reserve need not maximize bits minus a price times joules**. It may cost too much energy or harm neighboring links. That is a legitimate optimization trade-off. “It buys no reserve” is not.

## 3. Is your exact combination a defensible real design?

### The individual ingredients are reasonable; their composition is problematic

Causal MODCOD selection, rate-driven power control, implementation allowances, and fade back-off can each be sensible.

But your particular ordering creates the following deterministic consequence. For an uncapped exact-target solution,

$$
\gamma_{\mathrm{nom}}=\Gamma_{m_r}.
$$

With \(q<1\),

$$
q\gamma_{\mathrm{nom}}<\Gamma_{m_r}.
$$

Thus the intended mode is rejected by construction. If it was already the lowest available mode, the feasible-mode set is empty.

That conclusion follows from the controller equations; it does not require rain, a bad channel realization, or insufficient maximum RF power. The figure’s tiny floating-point PHY-floor guard does not materially alter it.

I would describe this as **controller-induced outage**, specifically **lowest-MODCOD starvation with load-induced service inversion**. These are descriptive names for the model’s behavior, not a DVB-defined operating feature.

### “Adding another user makes both servable” does not mean both meet the target

There is a stronger consequence.

If \(m_r\) is selected as the least-threshold mode satisfying

$$
\frac{B\eta_{m_r}}{n}\geq R^\star,
$$

then a mode with a strictly lower threshold cannot satisfy the same rate requirement under the same airtime; otherwise it would have been eligible as the original target.

Therefore, under that target-selection rule:

> **Every strict back-off from the uncapped target mode misses the target rate, even when decoding succeeds.**

For an illustration using the supplied table, take an **additional 1.7 dB fade back-off**, separate from the 1.7 dB implementation allowance:

| Occupancy | Target mode | Mode after back-off | Per-user rate when decoded |
| --------: | ----------- | ------------------- | -------------------------: |
|         1 | QPSK 1/4    | No valid mode       |                          0 |
|         2 | QPSK 2/5    | QPSK 1/4            |               34.04 Mbit/s |

The two-user case becomes decodable, but neither user achieves 50 Mbit/s. The apparent improvement comes from the occupancy-dependent controller permitting more RF expenditure—not from an improvement in the propagation channel.

Your v1.8 amendment explicitly calls 50 Mbit/s a **power-control setpoint, not a demand model**. That avoids claiming a hard rate guarantee that the design does not provide. It does not, however, make systematic starvation at low occupancy an ordinary consequence of Ka-band link physics. The exact package locations and calculations are recorded in the [numerical audit](sandbox:/mnt/data/r11b_audit/results/Round-11B-numerical-audit.md).

### Full-power transmission with no valid mode needs a separate justification

Under the behavior you describe, a transmission guaranteed to deliver zero useful bits still consumes energy and creates interference. **Absent a modeled signaling, synchronization, or future-service benefit, muting that data transmission is preferable for your instantaneous bits-minus-energy objective.**

Non-payload transmission is not inherently unrealistic: DVB-S2 specifies dummy frames. But that does not establish that every no-mode data opportunity must radiate at the cap. ([ETSI][5])

There is also a package-level ambiguity: your prompt says **full power**, whereas the controller finding says **computed power**. The actual engine implementation is not included. These can describe different cases:

* A target that is unreachable may lead the power solver to the cap.
* A margin-selected `NO_MODE` can occur after a much lower nominal power was computed.

If `NO_MODE` actually triggers a subsequent jump to full power, mode feasibility should be reconsidered at that new power. Otherwise the controller evaluates feasibility at one RF level and executes another.

**My adjudication:** I would not accept this composition as a normal rate-targeting fade-margin design with the current rationale. It could be retained as an explicitly constrained **no-boost baseline**, but “deliberately conservative” alone is insufficient: its imposed low-load starvation and distorted association incentives must be stated. I recommend fixing it rather than making it the primary physical model.

## 4. What the availability and link-budget numbers do—and do not—show

### The quoted 19.2/9.9 dB pair belongs to different cases

The supplied engine report, §4, line 36, assigns:

> 19.196747 dB to the 550-km boresight case, and 9.851770 dB to the 1,100-km half-power-edge case.

Those are not the figure’s fixed 2,000-km, 10° inputs. The underlying independent link-ledger program is referenced but absent, so its complete inputs cannot be reconciled here.

Using `figure1_compute.py`’s actual constants and equations gives:

| Quantity                               |    Boresight | Half-power edge |
| -------------------------------------- | -----------: | --------------: |
| Transmit antenna gain                  |   33.010 dBi |      30.000 dBi |
| EIRP at 1.65 W                         |   35.185 dBW |      32.175 dBW |
| Free-space loss at 20 GHz, 2,000 km    |   184.489 dB |      184.489 dB |
| Nominal atmospheric loss in the figure |     2.520 dB |        2.520 dB |
| System noise temperature               |    242.294 K |       242.294 K |
| Noise over 166.667 MHz                 | −122.537 dBW |    −122.537 dBW |
| **Nominal C/N at the cap**             | **5.714 dB** |    **2.703 dB** |

These are independent deterministic calculations from the supplied model, not a newly calibrated real-world propagation budget. The [audit and reproducible calculation bundle](sandbox:/mnt/data/Round-11B-numerical-audit.zip) contains the equations, inputs, outputs, and evidence locations.

As a basic consistency check, changing only from boresight to the half-power edge at the same distance and other losses produces approximately **3 dB**, not the approximately 9.35 dB difference between the report’s two different cases.

### The cap can support a reserved lone-user link in a concrete example

In the figure’s convention, the lowest-mode threshold is approximately

$$
\Gamma_{\min,\mathrm{dB}}=-1.442\ \mathrm{dB}.
$$

The available cap headroom above that threshold is therefore approximately **7.16 dB at boresight** and **4.15 dB at the edge**.

Consider an illustrative extra 3 dB fade reserve:

$$
q=10^{-3/10}\approx0.5012.
$$

The corresponding lone-user powers are:

| Case            | Nominal exact-threshold power | Power including 3 dB reserve |
| --------------- | ----------------------------: | ---------------------------: |
| Boresight       |                       0.318 W |                  **0.634 W** |
| Half-power edge |                       0.635 W |                  **1.268 W** |

Both remain below 1.65 W. The QPSK 1/4 payload rate in this fixture is approximately 68.09 Mbit/s when decoded.

**This is a counterexample to the proposition that a lone user must be unservable because of the physical cap.** It is not a claim that your unavailable actual \(q_{10}\) equals a 3 dB reserve. A deeper required reserve or significant interference could make the link infeasible.

### The aggregate figures need distinct interpretations

**The 62% `NO_MODE` rate is compatible with the pathological policy you describe.** Its existence does not validate a propagation explanation. Conversely, I cannot establish how much of that 62% is caused by low-load starvation without the underlying census and a decomposition by occupancy, power saturation, and channel condition.

**The 40.8% target-rate infeasibility may include genuine physical limitations.** For example, the corrected nominal edge budget supports only about 137.34 Mbit/s of beam throughput under the figure’s assumptions before additional fade reserve. Three equal-airtime users cannot each receive 50 Mbit/s from that capacity. This is different from being unable to decode any mode.

**The 0.453 availability cannot be judged from the listed power, distance, bandwidth, and antenna gain alone.** A propagation-based availability calculation requires geographic and atmospheric statistics, elevation exposure, and a defined service criterion. ITU-R P.618 explicitly requires inputs such as rain statistics, location, elevation, and frequency for its Earth–space attenuation prediction procedures. ([ITU][6])

There is also a useful accounting check:

$$
\text{same-population availability}
\leq 1-\text{NO\_MODE fraction},
$$

if every `NO_MODE` opportunity contributes zero availability and both quantities use the same weights and time units. With 62% `NO_MODE`, that upper bound is 0.38, not 0.453.

This **does not prove a numerical error**, because the package identifies 0.453138 as a paired two-anchor SMOKE result, while the 62% figure is reported as a census observation whose underlying data are absent. It does mean the populations and denominators must be reconciled before those numbers are interpreted together.

One additional normalization check is warranted: the figure already includes deterministic 1.08 dB scintillation in nominal gain, while v1.9 defines a fading-product quantile that also includes scintillation. **Check that the engine does not count the same factor twice.** The supplied bundle does not establish that it does; defining \(G=h_{\mathrm{real}}/h_{\mathrm{nominal}}\) consistently would resolve the question.

## 5. Should the higher-order modes be reachable?

**At the quoted 19.2/9.9 dB values, some higher-order modes should be reachable under suitable remaining margin and interference conditions. At the actual figure’s 5.71/2.70 dB values, they are not nominally reachable using the figure’s threshold convention.**

### First use compatible units

The standard’s reference thresholds are \(E_s/N_0\), not directly \(C/N\) over occupied bandwidth. Under your script’s convention,

$$
B=(1+\alpha)R_s,\qquad \alpha=0.20,
$$

and with its 1.7 dB implementation allowance,

$$
\Gamma_{m,C/N,\mathrm{dB}}
=
(E_s/N_0)_{m,\mathrm{ideal}}
+1.7-10\log_{10}(1.20).
$$

Thus the adjustment to the ideal table is approximately \(+0.9082\) dB. Using the reference values in DVB-S2 Table 13 gives: ([ETSI][5])

| Mode        | Required C/N under your convention |
| ----------- | ---------------------------------: |
| QPSK 1/4    |                          −1.442 dB |
| 8PSK 3/5    |                           6.408 dB |
| 8PSK 2/3    |                           7.528 dB |
| 8PSK 3/4    |                           8.818 dB |
| 16APSK 2/3  |                           9.878 dB |
| 32APSK 3/4  |                          13.638 dB |
| 32APSK 9/10 |                          16.958 dB |

These values include the stated implementation allowance, **not an additional fade reserve or cochannel interference penalty**.

### Conditional answer for 19.2/9.9 dB

At **19.2 dB**, even 32APSK 9/10 is feasible under these simplified assumptions, with roughly 2.24 dB remaining before any additional fade reserve.

At **9.9 dB**, 8PSK is clearly relevant. The lowest 16APSK entry is only marginally feasible. In fact, the report’s unrounded **9.851770 dB is slightly below the 9.878188 dB threshold**, so rounding to 9.9 changes that particular decision.

These are calculated possibilities, not hardware performance guarantees. The standard’s reference values are idealized; actual channel and equipment impairments must be included. ([ETSI][5])

### Answer for the actual 2,000-km figure

At boresight,

$$
5.714<6.408\ \mathrm{dB},
$$

so even the lowest-threshold 8PSK mode is unavailable under the figure’s implementation allowance. The edge is weaker still. Adding a positive fade reserve cannot change that in favor of higher-order modulation.

Therefore:

> **The QPSK-only table is a specification/completeness error, but it is not evidence that higher-order throughput was artificially removed from this particular fixed-geometry figure.**

Correcting the table remains necessary. It changes the modeled capability envelope and behavior at other geometries. The table-only beam-rate ceiling rises from approximately **248.42 to 618.48 Mbit/s**, corresponding to four versus twelve 50-Mbit/s users at sufficiently high SINR. Those are **mode-table feasibility limits**, not proof that twelve users are feasible at the stated power cap and geometry.

Also, the 28-mode table is the base DVB-S2 catalogue in question, **not the complete DVB-S2X mode set**; S2X adds further capabilities. That distinction should be explicit in the specification. ([ETSI][2])

### Extending the table also requires fixing the selection algorithm

The figure sorts modes by threshold and returns the last feasible entry. That works for its QPSK-only subset but does not generally maximize throughput across all 28 entries.

For example, **16APSK 2/3 has a lower reference threshold and higher spectral efficiency than 8PSK 5/6**. Threshold order is therefore not throughput order. ([ETSI][5])

For a highest-throughput ACM decision, use

$$
m_{\mathrm{tx}}
=
\arg\max_{m:\,\Gamma_m\leq\gamma_{\mathrm{safe}}}\eta_m,
$$

with explicit tie-breaking—not “last feasible threshold” or “largest mode index.”

## 6. What I would amend before the main run

**Preserve the causal correction:** choose the transmitted mode from information available before transmission, then credit its bits only when the realized channel decodes it. Do not revert to selecting credited MODCOD retrospectively from realized SINR.

**Remove the prohibition justified by “power margin buys no reserve.”** For your existing rate-target controller, the coherent construction is to first test whether the target mode can be supported with the declared reserve:

$$
q_i h_{ii}p_i
\geq
\Gamma_{m_{r,i}}
\left(N_i+\sum_{j\neq i}h_{ji}p_j\right),
$$

subject to RF and payload constraints. This equation is appropriate when its treatment of interference matches the reliability model; otherwise the constraint must incorporate the relevant interference uncertainty.

When that target is infeasible, explicitly distinguish **lower-rate service**, **rate-target failure**, and **no data service**. A lower mode at capped power is a reasonable fallback; continuing zero-payload RF needs its own modeled purpose. Fixed-power, margin-aware ACM is also a legitimate alternative, but it should be implemented as that design rather than through a nominal exact-threshold power loop.

Before re-deriving the figure, reconcile its geometry with the link ledger, implement the complete declared mode set with a correct feasible-mode selection rule, and verify that nominal gain and fading quantiles use the same normalization.

**Bottom line:** amend the rule, not just its wording. Your power-margin objection is valid. The lowest-mode starvation is a controller artifact, although genuine rate infeasibility may coexist with it. The measured low availability should not be presented as a Ka-band link-budget result until those effects have been separated.
