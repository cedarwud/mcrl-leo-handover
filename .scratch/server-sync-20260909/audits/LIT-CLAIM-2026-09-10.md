**Q1: What the catfish source establishes**

The supplied CDRL document is Bo-Han Ke’s July 2025 master’s thesis, not a paper establishing a general acceptance standard. Its abstract says the framework “comprises two agents: the catfish agent and the main agent.” Its three mechanisms belong to that two-agent design; they are not three separately deployed catfish agents. The motivation is an introduced competitor that stimulates exploration: the catfish agent collects “rare, high-reward experiences,” and periodically supplies them to the main agent. The transfer is “experience-level knowledge transfer.” At intervention, the main training batch contains 70% main replay and 30% catfish replay. At deployment, the main actor produces actions and the catfish actor monitors performance. (C, Abstract; §§3.3, 4.2.2, 4.3, pp.21–22, 30–39.)

| Mechanism | Intended role in CDRL | What its leave-one-out ablation actually changes |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Experience stratification | Creates an “elite challenger role”: high-EE experiences train the catfish; intermediate-EE experiences train the main agent. The high threshold is the top 20% of generated data; experiences below the top-50% threshold are discarded. | Replaces stratification with “an unsegmented experience-replay buffer.” This changes the training-data distribution, not a physical state input or an inference head. |
| 2. Asymmetric discounting | Separates short-term and long-term learning objectives to “avoid strategy homogenization.” The main agent emphasizes immediate demands; the catfish emphasizes future returns. | Uses “an identical discount factor,” γM = γCF = 0.95. This changes the learning target/horizon, not the number of agents. The strategy-only arm uses 0.95 and 0.99. |
| 3. Adaptive competitive reward | Rewards the catfish for outperforming the main agent and penalizes it for trailing: rC = rCF + η(rCF − rM). It supports the challenger’s performance lead. | The catfish “receives no extra reward.” This removes the additional training signal, not the environment reward, an observation input, or the whole catfish agent. |

Sources for the table: C, §4.2.2(A–C), pp.33–37, Eqs.(4.8)–(4.10); §5.3, p.51. These are functional training interventions. The thesis does not document an equal-compute, equal-data-volume placebo protocol for them.

The experiment measures contribution in **two distinct ways**. First, it compares FULL against FULL minus one strategy: “In each experiment, one strategy is removed from the complete CDRL scheme.” Second, it plots three strategy-only arms alongside the basic actor–critic baseline, labelled “AC active RIS.” Thus the leave-one-out reference is **complete CDRL**; the strategy-only reference is **basic AC**, not nearest association, a converged optimization oracle, or hardware with an RF chain physically removed. The main performance section separately compares CDRL with AC, BPOF, and teacher–student learning, and separately contrasts switchable and all-on RIS hardware. Those hardware comparisons are not the three training ablations. (C, §§5.2–5.3, pp.41–42, 51–52, Fig.5.7.)

The ablation’s headline is total EE. The caption and vertical axis of Fig.5.7 say energy efficiency, although §5.3 mistakenly calls the experiment “Transmission rate vs. transmission power constraint.” The text describes a moderate loss without stratification, a larger loss without asymmetric discounting, and the **“smallest effect”** without competitive reward, “implying that the baseline reward density already sufficiently drives CDRL training.” It calls the reward’s benefit “limited.” (C, §5.3, pp.51–52.)

At the 20-dBm endpoint, Fig.5.7 visually gives FULL ≈46.6, without strategy 1 ≈45.7, without strategy 2 ≈45.3, and without strategy 3 ≈46.3 bit/Hz/J: roughly **+2%, +3%, and +0.6%**, respectively, for FULL relative to each ablation. The AC baseline is ≈43.4; strategy-only arms are ≈44.2, 44.6, and 43.6, respectively. The third strategy’s standalone gain is therefore only about **0.5%** at that endpoint. These are approximate illustrations, not tabulated margins. The plot has no uncertainty bars or reported per-component significance test. (C, §5.3, Fig.5.7, p.52.)

**Does C report a zero or negative component contribution? No explicit such result is reported.** Its plotted ordering supports positive effects at the illustrated endpoint, with competitive reward contributing very little. “Smallest effect” and “limited benefit” must not be rewritten as “zero,” “harmful,” or proof of a statistically positive gain everywhere. The text also contains two presentation inconsistencies: its strategy-2/3 color descriptions disagree with the figure legend, and its final strategy-only explanation says “without” competitive reward after defining that arm as using only that reward mechanism. The interpretation above follows the named strategy definitions and figure legend; the thesis does not resolve those inconsistencies. (C, §5.3, pp.51–52.)

**The owner’s requirement is stricter than the source’s demonstrated bar.** The source’s phrase “all aimed at ensuring its policy outperforms that of the main agent” describes the joint design intent of the catfish’s three mechanisms. It does not state that each must independently deliver a specified positive margin in system EE. Its evidence does go in the positive direction in both ablation styles, so it would also be inaccurate to say C provides no individual-effect evidence. What the owner adds is a mandatory quantitative acceptance threshold for every component, transferred to the project’s different system and power model. C itself already uses a summed-rate/total-power ratio. If “individually” instead means three independent deployed controllers or additive gains that must sum, that is also a different architectural/causal claim: neither ablation establishes additivity. (C, §3.2, Eqs.(3.12a), (3.13a); §4.2.2, p.30; §5.3, pp.51–52; Chapter 6, p.54.)

The literature does not cancel that project requirement. Relaxing it would be a project decision that must be stated openly; it cannot be attributed to C. Conversely, a component can legitimately serve a supporting learning role without satisfying the owner’s chosen margin, but that would support a narrower claim than the existing acceptance requirement. This is the methodological consequence of the source’s design and ablation distinctions above.

**Q2: The comparison evidence, with the comparators kept intact**

“Joint,” “distributed,” and “iterative” are not opposites in these sources. Several proposed coordinated algorithms are themselves sequential or iterative. None of the twelve PDFs reports the exact project comparison: simultaneous multi-user association moves versus the same objective optimized by unilateral moves to a certified fixed point. The ledger below covers the relevant comparison families, giving reported magnitudes or representative figure readings rather than inventing a single aggregate percentage where the source provides only curves.

**Y — User association and load balancing (2013).** This is the most useful warning against confusing a weak-baseline gain with a coordination premium. It optimizes sum-log user rates at fixed BS powers; its equal-allocation result is relevant to multiplexing, but it is not the project’s rate-target power controller. Moreover, “joint association” means that users “can be associated with more than one BS at the same time,” not that several users change single-BS associations together. Its distributed algorithm coordinates choices through iterated prices; it is not a unilateral best-response certificate. (Y, §§II, III-B–D, IV-B, pp.2707–2711.)

**The blunt answer is not “the literature guarantees a large coordination gain.”** The closest local-search evidence is modest: U’s ≈1.2% benefit from annealing beyond exchange-stable greedy matching; Y’s nearly overlapping optimized associations; T’s common centralized/distributed endpoint. B even supplies a ≈0.2–0.3% marginal coordination regime. But the corpus also does **not** establish the universal proposition that joint gains are generally small after any good unilateral algorithm converges. Those algorithms, move neighborhoods, metrics, and environments differ. The justified statement is narrower: **large residual coordination gains are not established here, and several relevant examples warn against assuming them.** (Y, §VI-B; T, §VII; U, §IV-D; B, §V.2, quoted above.)

For this project, the supplied **+0.51%** measures the residual benefit of the tested bounded joint search over the certified unilateral fixed point. The **+442%** measures the benefit of that load/objective-aware unilateral optimizer over geometry-only association. They answer different questions. Neither measures the causal contribution of any learned component; neither establishes a global joint optimum. A bounded search’s observed advantage is not an upper bound on every possible coordinated policy. These are logical limits of the supplied measurements, not additional literature results.

**Is speed a recognized contribution? Yes, within this corpus.** T presents reduced over-the-air iteration overhead; H includes acquisition overhead in delivered rate; D explicitly advocates “offline training + online inference”; U chooses the cheaper stable-matching variant and permits a quality/complexity tradeoff. A even presents faster convergence alongside a claimed small throughput sacrifice, albeit with contradictory plotting and no measured latency. These support a claim such as **achieving useful decision quality within the operational interval**, provided it is demonstrated. They do not support replacing a small endpoint gap with an unmeasured speed claim. (T, §VII, Fig.8; H, §§I, II-B, V; D, §V-C; U, §IV-B, IV-D; A, §IV-B.)

**My referee-facing judgment:** comparing what can be delivered by a genuine decision deadline is legitimate. Presenting a deliberately ineffective implementation as the strongest available deadline-feasible alternative would be flattering the method. The distinction rests on the protocol and evidence, not on whether the comparison happens to favor learning. No supplied paper establishes a referee rule about preregistration or guarantees acceptance; the following implications are methodological judgments from the stated project facts and the precedents above.

1. **Use the actual 30.08-second allowance.** A five-second version achieving nothing says nothing decisive about what it achieves at 30.08 seconds. An average 65-second convergence time shows full convergence is too slow on average for one interval; it does not give the deadline-miss distribution or the quality of an incumbent available earlier. The supplied facts do not yet establish a win at the proposed budget.
2. **Define the feasible result returned at the deadline.** A method that maintains useful intermediate associations must receive credit for them; if interrupted, its valid incumbent or predefined fallback is the output. Warm starts and incremental updates should receive a fair opportunity if they are available under the same information and operational rules. A comparison against “no action until complete convergence” needs a reason that behavior is intrinsic or deployment-relevant.
3. **Make the clock and information rules comparable.** State hardware/resources, what preprocessing is amortized, and whether action construction, feasibility checks, communication, and actuation consume the interval. Separate offline learning cost from online inference. Preserve the specified pooled-EE denominator; reporting computational energy additionally is different from silently changing that metric. T demonstrates why computing cost can matter, while D distinguishes training from inference.
4. **Address valid advance scheduling.** B explicitly computes ahead when its overhead exceeds one frame. Therefore 65 seconds versus 30.08 seconds alone does not prove an iterative method is undeployable: the project must justify whether forecasting/pipelining is valid for its state and information. This is not a recommendation to give any method future truth; the same causal information restrictions must apply.
5. **Retain the converged comparison.** Report the +0.51% endpoint diagnostic and +442% geometry-baseline diagnostic alongside the deadline result. A deadline advantage would establish timely decision quality, not a large irreducible advantage of joint association or independent EE benefits from all three learned components.

**Is the prior contract sufficient mitigation? Not by itself for everything now proposed.** Accepting the user’s stated chronology, it is sufficient to rebut the specific accusation that the **budgeted comparison itself was invented after seeing these results**. It is not sufficient to establish that the comparator is fair, that the result is positive, or that the comparison was already primary. If the contract explicitly fixed this budgeted endpoint as primary—or fixed an outcome-independent rule making it primary—before the results, following that rule is legitimate. If it merely listed the comparison as secondary or optional, promoting it now because the converged gap is small is still an outcome-informed change in claim priority. It may be transparently reported as a changed or exploratory emphasis, but not called a pre-specified primary success. The supplied context establishes prior inclusion, not the exact priority clause; this reading task does not authenticate the contract’s version history.

The legitimate potential claim is therefore **“the system attains a specified pooled EE within the real decision interval, relative to credible methods under the same operational constraints.”** The present measurements do not yet establish that claim, and it would still require individual ablations if the owner retains the three-component margin requirement. The corpus supports asking that question; it supplies no exemption from answering it with evidence.
